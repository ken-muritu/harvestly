02 The problem

The sack doesn’t tell you.

Day 0. The harvest comes in. It looks fine.

These two sacks hold maize from the same field. One is at 13% moisture. The other is at 18%. You cannot tell which is which by looking. Moisture is invisible, and the weather changes it every day.

How long maize keeps, at 18 °C

  1. 15% moisture 213 storage days
  2. 19% moisture 30 storage days
  3. 23% moisture 11 storage days

One tick is one day: storage period to about 0.5% dry-matter loss, University of Arkansas Extension FSA1058, Table 1 7. The East African standard limit for maize is 13.5% moisture 3. These are US corn tables; see the limits.

04 One week, replayed

What happens in a week.

The engine’s demo run on simulated weather, replayed one day at a time from its reference results. An illustration, not a result. Scroll, and the days pass.

What the replay assumes

The fall from 261 days to 34 depends on our assumption that a sack closes 5% of the gap to the air’s moisture each day. That rate has not been tested in the field. The count starts at the first test, not at harvest.

Countdown level is the engine’s own name (Low, Medium, High) for how soon the storage time runs out. It does not say the grain is dry: wet grain can have a long countdown, so moisture is shown on its own line.

The printed page shows the first day of the replay only. In short: on day 3 the forecast turned damp and the countdown fell from 261 days to 34, on simulated weather; on day 12 a meter reading cleared the grain and it was sealed. The day-by-day replay needs the live page.

      07 What we know

      What we know, and what we do not.

      Every number in the engine carries a label. They are listed on How it works, with their sources on Sources. The limits, once:

      • The pilot has not run. Nothing on this site is a result. No farmer has been enrolled; the test bot answers whoever messages it, on simulated weather.
      • 13.5% is the limit for sale, not a year’s guarantee. It is the East African standard limit 3. Arkansas guidance for keeping corn for a full year is 12% 6. A countdown of 365 days means 365 or more at the weather entered.
      • The storage clock starts at the first test, not at harvest. The source counts from harvest, so grain that sat wet before its first test has already used part of its life.
      • The tables are for US corn. Kenyan white maize has a different moisture curve 36, and we have not validated against it. The moisture table reads about 0.6–0.7 points wetter than measured maize points 19, which errs toward a false alarm. Below 15% moisture the storage-days figure is extended past its table and flagged.
      • The salt test is a coarse screen at about 15% 18. It asks for non-iodised salt, and Kenyan table salt is normally iodised. We do not know yet what that does.
      • Two simplifications. Daily air temperature stands in for grain temperature. Insects are not modelled: the engine sends one reminder at week 7 and points to hermetic bags.
      • Status, 12 October 2026. The engine exists as a tested Python reference. A Telegram test bot (@HarvestlyBot) answers with simulated weather; it replies only after you message it and sends no unprompted alerts. The Oracle version, SMS, USSD and the dashboard are planned, not built.

      08 Where it goes next

      The warehouse gate.

      13.5% is also the intake line used by the National Cereals and Produce Board (NCPB) and the warehouse receipt system 317. Depots with driers may accept wetter grain and dry it, so the line matters most for grain that goes to a depot without one. Grain that leaves the farm already measured and dry meets it before it gets there.

      A pilot is proposed for Trans Nzoia, October 2026 to January 2027. It is a plan, not a pledge, and it has not started. Read the pilot plan
